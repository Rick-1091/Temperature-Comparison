Make weather expectations easier to understand

(1) Understand weather signals first

When facing uncertain weather, users first need to understand three things: the most likely weather, how dispersed expectations are, and how closely past expectations matched actual outcomes.

Our visualization brings official weather information, Polymarket prices for different temperature ranges, and historical observations into a unified view. Users can see the temperature range currently favored by the market and use the relative price distribution across ranges to assess whether market expectations are concentrated or dispersed, then consider official forecasts and warnings.

These data provide supporting information, not a certain answer; viewing the information does not require trading.

(2) Turn uncertainty into preparation

The value of information is not simply knowing tomorrow’s temperature, but helping users decide in advance whether to adjust their plans.

For example, when rainfall risk rises or different signals disagree substantially, a household relying on outdoor drying can prepare covered space, shorten each drying session, or process grain in batches. Tourism and waterfront businesses can also adjust outdoor activities accordingly.

The interface therefore focuses on:

Possible weather → Concentration of expectations → Historical performance → Flexibility in timing and space

The system does not make decisions for users. It makes probabilities, distributions, and forecast errors easier to understand, so weather uncertainty can better inform practical planning.

(3) Offer another signal when usual weather-information channels are limited

In some regions or circumstances, radio, television, or local weather information may be temporarily unavailable. If users still have internet access and a relatively active prediction market exists for the relevant place and date, Polymarket can offer a supplementary information signal based on market participants’ collective expectations.

Users can review:

Currently favored temperature range → Distribution of market support across outcomes → Historical comparisons of similar expectations with final results

This information can help users form a rough judgment with explicit uncertainty boundaries rather than relying entirely on one source.

However, prediction-market prices are affected by participation, liquidity, trading behavior, and market rules, and are not equivalent to forecast probabilities issued by professional meteorological agencies. Historical NOAA observations may also differ from the market’s actual settlement criteria. For safety-related weather events such as heavy rain, extreme heat, or typhoons, official warnings from local meteorological and emergency agencies should remain the primary reference. In non-emergency situations, our goal is not to replace weather forecasts with Polymarket, but to build an easier-to-understand comparison interface across weather information sources, helping ordinary users see what is predicted, how much disagreement exists, and how expectations performed in the past.
